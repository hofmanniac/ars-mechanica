## Overview

This combination merges drafting's selection strategy with set collection's completion goals, creating interactive gameplay where you must both build your sets and deny opponents theirs.

## How It Works

Drafting and collecting work together in structured phases:

**Draft phase** (typically):
1. Deal hands of 7-10 cards to each player
2. Each player selects 1 card, passes rest to neighbor
3. Repeat until hands are depleted
4. Multiple draft rounds create larger collections

**Collection phase**:
- Cards drafted accumulate in your collection
- Score points for completing sets during or at end
- Set types: runs (sequences), groups (matching), colors, symbols

**Strategic considerations**:
- **Greedy drafting**: Take best cards for your sets
- **Hate drafting**: Take cards opponents need (deny them)
- **Flexible drafting**: Take cards that fit multiple sets
- **Signaling**: Your picks reveal your strategy to neighbors

## Why It Works

The combination creates engaging player interaction:

- **Tension in every pick**: Help yourself or hurt opponents?
- **Information game**: Read signals about neighbor strategies
- **Adaptation**: Pivot if your desired cards aren't coming
- **Grudge picks**: Satisfying to deny opponents key cards
- **Multiple paths**: Various set types support different strategies

Pure set collection can be multi-player solitaire. Drafting adds competition and forces players to react to each other.

## Design Considerations

**Draft pool size**: Hands of 7-10 cards work well. Smaller hands (5-6) limit choices; larger hands (12+) create too much information to track around the table.

**Pass direction**: Alternate pass directions between rounds (left, right, across) to distribute cards more evenly and reduce "downstream" advantage.

**Card distribution**: Ensure enough cards of each set type that multiple players can pursue similar strategies without complete denial. 2-3x cards needed for a set.

**Set scoring balance**: Multiple small sets vs. few large sets should score comparably. Otherwise dominant strategy emerges.

**Hate-drafting incentive**: If sets are so abundant that denial doesn't matter, drafting becomes automatic. Scarcity creates interesting picks.

**Visible information**: Should completed sets be visible (public info) or hidden (secret goals)? Public creates informed blocking; hidden adds guessing.

**Draft count**: 2-3 draft rounds is typical. Single draft feels too short; 4+ rounds drags.

**Player count scaling**: More players = longer draft passes = more uncertainty about what comes around. 3-5 players optimal; 6+ gets chaotic.

## Examples in Games

This combination is the foundation of many drafting games where players collect cards to form sets, famously seen in party games and lighter strategy games with quick drafting phases.
