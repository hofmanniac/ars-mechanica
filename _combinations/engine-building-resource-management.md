## Overview

This foundational combination forms the economic heart of many engine-building games. Players construct production chains that generate resources, convert them efficiently, and ultimately transform them into victory conditions. The engine is explicitly an economic system — inputs become outputs, with each added component improving throughput, efficiency, or variety.

## How It Works

Players build resource generation and conversion chains:

**Engine Components**:

**Resource Generators**
- "Produce 2 wood each round"
- "Gain 1 gold when you build a building"
- Provide raw materials for the economy

**Resource Converters**
- "Trade 2 wood for 3 gold"
- "Spend 1 ore to gain 2 iron + 1 coin"
- Transform abundant resources into scarce ones

**Conversion Enhancers**
- "When you trade, gain +1 resource of your choice"
- "Resource conversion costs 1 less"
- Make conversions more efficient

**Resource Multipliers**
- "Gain 1 resource for each market you own"
- "Production doubled if you control the harbor"
- Scale production with engine size

**Storage/Capacity**
- Warehouses, vaults, or expansion allowing resource accumulation
- Bottleneck management

**Economic Cycle**:
1. **Production Phase**: Generators produce resources
2. **Conversion Phase**: Transform resources through conversion chains
3. **Investment Phase**: Spend resources to acquire new engine components
4. **Victory Phase**: Convert resources/engine to points (end-game or ongoing)

## Why It Works

This combination creates deeply engaging economic gameplay:

**Tangible Progression**
- Resource production visibly increases each round
- Players see direct results of their investments
- Satisfying feeling of abundance after early scarcity

**Optimization Puzzle**
- Finding efficient resource conversion paths
- Identifying bottlenecks in production chains
- Mathematical optimization (players who enjoy math love this)

**Strategic Diversification**
- Multiple resource types create branching paths
- Can specialize (focus on one chain) or diversify (many small chains)
- Different strategies emerge based on available components

**Clear Cause-and-Effect**
- Direct connection between actions and results
- Easy to understand why choices succeed or fail
- Teaches systems thinking naturally

**Compounding Returns**
- Each added component benefits from existing infrastructure
- Later investments are more powerful than early ones
- Rewards planning and patience

## Design Considerations

**Resource Types**

How many resource types should exist?
- **Few (2-3)**: Simple, easy to track, limited strategic depth
- **Many (6+)**: Complex, requires more tracking, rich optimization space
- **Sweet spot**: 4-5 distinct resources provides depth without overwhelming

**Resource Function**
- **Generic**: Resources are interchangeable (boring but simple)
- **Specialized**: Each resource has specific uses (interesting but complex)
- **Tiered**: Basic→Advanced→Premium resources (clear progression)
- **Wildcard**: Some resources act as "any type" (flexibility valve)

**Production Balance**

How fast do engines generate resources?
- **Slow burn**: 1-2 resources per turn early, 5-8 late (long game, patient strategy)
- **Fast ramp**: Quick production increases (shorter game, immediate gratification)
- **Exponential**: Production doubles/triples (exciting but risky for balance)

**Conversion Rates**

What are fair exchange rates?
- **Equal exchanges**: 1 wood = 1 gold (simple but lacks strategic depth)
- **Unequal exchanges**: 2 wood = 1 gold (creates resource hierarchy)
- **Variable rates**: Exchange rates change based on market/engine (dynamic optimization)
- **Balance**: Ensure no resource is always superior

**Storage Limits**

Should resources be limited?
- **Unlimited storage**: Simplifies tracking, allows hoarding
- **Limited storage**: Adds pressure to spend, creates waste avoidance puzzle
- **Expandable storage**: Storage is part of engine building
- **Trade-off**: Limits add interesting decisions but increase complexity

**Resource Sinks**

Where do resources go?
- **Victory points**: Direct conversion (simple, but can feel disconnected from theme)
- **Building costs**: Resources buy engine components (thematic, strategic)
- **Maintenance**: Upkeep costs for engines (tension, prevents runaway)
- **Multiple sinks**: Different paths to utilize resources (strategic variety)

**Engine Startup**

How long before engines pay off?
- **Immediate returns**: Components produce same turn (fast feedback)
- **Next round**: Acquire this turn, produce next turn (standard)
- **Delayed returns**: 2-3 rounds before production (risky investment)
- **Game length**: Must align with startup time (game must last long enough)

**Catch-Up Mechanisms**

Preventing runaway leaders:
- **Diminishing returns**: Each production increase costs more
- **Scaling costs**: Better components cost exponentially more
- **Production caps**: Maximum production per round
- **Helping hand**: Trailing players get bonuses
- **Goal**: Reward good play but keep competition alive

## Strategic Layers

**Economic Efficiency**
- Identifying cheapest paths to desired resources
- Minimizing waste in conversion chains
- Optimizing production-to-conversion ratios

**Investment Timing**
- When to expand production vs. conversion
- Balancing immediate resources vs. long-term growth
- Recognizing when to stop building and harvest

**Bottleneck Analysis**
- Finding limiting factors in resource chains
- Prioritizing which bottlenecks to address first
- Understanding which resources gate victory

**Portfolio Management**
- Diversification (many resource types) vs. specialization (one chain)
- Risk assessment (all-in on one strategy or hedge bets)
- Adaptation when key resources unavailable

**Opportunity Cost**
- Every resource spent is resources not spent elsewhere
- Evaluating competing investments
- Short-term needs vs. long-term engine building

## Common Pitfalls

**Resource Bloat**: Too many resource types = complicated tracking and cognitive overload. Keep to 4-6 maximum.

**Obvious Optimal Path**: If one resource chain is clearly best, no strategic decisions exist. Balance production and conversion values carefully.

**Accounting Tedium**: Tracking many small resource changes slows game. Use batch updates or production tracks.

**Math-Heavy**: If players need calculators to optimize, accessibility suffers. Keep numbers manageable (1-10 range).

**Runaway Engines**: Strong early engines dominate. Include catch-up mechanisms or engine costs that scale.

**Boring End Game**: Once engine built, turns become mechanical. Include late-game pivots or escalating challenges.

## Examples in Games

**Terraforming Mars**: Build production engines for 6 resource types (money, steel, titanium, plants, energy, heat). Cards provide ongoing production increases, conversion abilities, and synergies.

**Splendor**: Gem cards provide permanent discounts (resource converters). Each acquired card makes future acquisitions cheaper, creating an escalating economic engine.

**Century: Spice Road**: Pure conversion chain game. Cards convert spices (yellow→red→green→brown) with increasing efficiency. Build a conversion engine to produce valuable spice combinations.

**Gizmos**: Build machines that generate energy (resources) automatically when specific triggers occur (playing cards, converting, etc.). Energy converts to card plays, which trigger more energy production.
