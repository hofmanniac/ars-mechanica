## What This Dimension Represents

Conflict Resolution determines how contested situations are decided. It answers: **"When players compete for something, how is the winner determined?"**

This dimension shapes the feel of competition and confrontation. Dice create dramatic uncertainty. Card play rewards planning and hand management. Deterministic systems enable perfect calculation. Whether conflicts destroy resources or simply redistribute them affects strategic incentives.

Not all games have direct conflict, but most have some form of competition or contested outcomes. Your choice determines whether conflict feels like a risky gamble, a strategic puzzle, or a dramatic showdown.

## Common Mechanics

These are the most frequently used approaches to resolving conflicts:

### Dice Combat
Roll dice to determine combat outcomes. Classic and intuitive, creates drama and excitement. Can be modified by stats, cards, or abilities. Ranges from pure luck to risk management.

### Card-Based Combat
Play cards from hand to determine strength or abilities. Rewards planning, hand management, and resource conservation. More predictable than dice but still has hidden information tension.

### Bidding / Auction
Players secretly commit resources, higher bid wins. Economic resolution mechanic. Creates interesting risk/reward decisions and resource management tension.

### Deterministic Comparison
Compare known values, higher wins. Perfect information, no randomness. Pure strategic calculation. Used in abstract games and some area control games.

### Rock-Paper-Scissors
Circular superiority relationships between options. No universally best choice. Creates psychological metagame and prediction gameplay.

## Uncommon Implementations

These creative approaches to conflict resolution demonstrate alternative design space:

### Programmed Combat
**Example: RoboRally**
Pre-commit to sequence of actions, then resolve simultaneously. Planning without perfect knowledge of opponent choices. Chaos from collision.

### Bluffing Bids
**Example: Cosmic Encounter**
Players reveal combat cards simultaneously after negotiation opportunity. Social dynamics around trust and deception. Negotiation as conflict resolution.

### Attrition vs. Victory
**Example: Kemet**
Lose troops but don't lose position. Conflict redistributes forces rather than eliminating. Makes fighting less punitive and more tactical.

### Shared Loss
**Example: Condottiere**
Winner of battle claims objective but all participants lose resources. Victory is costly. Creates interesting decisions about when to fight.

### Defensive Advantage
**Example: Small World**
Defenders have inherent advantages making conquest costly. Rewards defensive positioning. Prevents constant aggression.

### Escalating Commitment
**Example: Battle for Rokugan**
Can commit additional resources mid-conflict. Bluffing about strength and pot commitment like poker. Psychological warfare.

## Dimensional Intersections

Conflict Resolution shapes competitive dynamics across dimensions:

### × Player Interaction
Resolution method determines how confrontational gameplay feels.
- Dice Combat = Dramatic but luck-based
- Deterministic = Calculated and strategic

### × Uncertainty & Information
Resolution type balances calculation vs. risk management.
- Perfect Information Combat = Chess-like tactics
- Hidden Resources = Bluffing and deception

### × Resource Economy
Conflict consumes or redistributes resources.
- Costly Combat = Strategic resource drain
- Winner-Take-All = High stakes battles

### × Action Selection
Conflict mechanics affect action incentives.
- Punitive Combat = Avoid conflict
- Rewarding Combat = Aggressive play

## Design Questions

When working with this dimension, consider:

- **How much luck should there be?** Do you want surprising upsets or calculable outcomes?
- **What do conflicts cost?** Are they resource drains, redistributions, or mostly free?
- **Can weaker players win?** Should underdogs have chances or should strength guarantee victory?
- **How long do conflicts take?** Are they quick resolution or extended engagements?
- **What role does prediction play?** Do players guess opponent choices or know everything?

## Patterns & Pitfalls

### Successful Patterns

**Modified Randomness**
Base resolution on dice but allow modifications through resources or abilities. Balances excitement with player agency. Example: Dice plus cards.

**Simultaneous Reveal**
Both players commit secretly, then reveal together. Creates tension and prevents perfect response. Example: Battle cards.

**Comeback Mechanics**
Losing conflicts provides compensation or advantages. Balances game through conflict outcomes. Example: Gaining resources from defeats.

**Graduated Outcomes**
Not just win/lose but degrees of victory. Close battles have different results than dominating wins. Creates more nuanced strategy.

**Opt-Out Options**
Players can choose to avoid conflict through alternate paths. Prevents forced confrontation. Example: Retreat or pay-to-avoid mechanics.

### Common Pitfalls

**Pure Luck Domination**
Combat outcomes entirely random regardless of preparation. Players feel powerless. Need ways to influence or mitigate randomness.

**Analysis Paralysis**
Too many factors make calculating optimal strategy overwhelming. Need bounded complexity or accept approximation.

**Runaway Military**
Early combat victories compound into unstoppable advantages. Need catch-up mechanics or diminishing returns on military.

**Constant Conflict Exhaustion**
Conflicts are so frequent or important that game becomes only about fighting. Other strategies become irrelevant. Need balanced incentives.

**Kingmaker Combat**
Weakened players determine winner by choosing who to attack. Breaks competitive integrity. Need mechanisms to guide or prevent this.

**Tedious Resolution**
Combat takes so long that it bogs down gameplay. Frequent fights become tedious. Need streamlined resolution for common conflicts.

## Examples in Practice

**Scythe** uses simultaneous card reveal combined with base combat power. Deterministic once revealed but secret selection creates tension. Combat is rare and meaningful.

**Twilight Imperium** has dice-based space combat with numerous modifying abilities. Lots of dice create statistical consistency while maintaining drama. Supports tactical decisions through positioning.

**Root** has simple deterministic combat - higher roll plus modifications wins. Very quick resolution. Focuses on strategic positioning rather than battle tactics.

**Blood Rage** uses card-based combat where players simultaneously reveal cards from drafted hands. Hand management and prediction crucial. Dying can be strategically valuable.

**Battle Line** makes conflict resolution into a series of tactical card games. Each battle is a micro-game of set-making. No randomness, pure hand management.

**Risk** uses pure dice rolling for combat. Simple and fast but highly random. Enables dramatic upsets and memorable moments but frustrates strategists.

**Game of Thrones: The Board Game** uses simultaneous order selection with support mechanics. Bluffing, alliances, and prediction matter more than dice. Highly political and strategic.
