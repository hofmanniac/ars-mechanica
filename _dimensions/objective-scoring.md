## What This Dimension Represents

Objective & Scoring defines what players are trying to achieve. It answers: **"How do you win this game?"**

This dimension shapes player motivation and strategic focus. Clear objectives guide decision-making. Multiple scoring paths create strategic diversity. Hidden objectives enable surprise and deduction. The victory condition fundamentally determines what players care about and optimize toward.

Every game needs a way to determine winners. Your choice shapes whether players focus narrowly on one goal or balance multiple objectives, whether they compete directly or race against the clock.

## Common Mechanics

These are the most frequently used approaches to objectives and scoring:

### Victory Points
Abstract points accumulated through various actions. Most common modern board game victory condition. Allows multiple paths to victory and flexible strategy. Can suffer from "point salad" where everything scores.

### Race to Objective
First player to achieve a specific goal wins. Creates urgency and direct competition. Clear focus but can feel arbitrary. Common in racing games and some deck-builders.

### Elimination
Last player remaining wins, or specific opponent defeat. Creates dramatic confrontation but problematic player elimination. Used in classic games, less common in modern designs.

### Set Collection
Achieve specific combinations or collections to score. Goals can be public or secret. Creates focused objectives and strategic card management. Classic in rummy-style games.

### Territory Control
Control majority of locations, regions, or resources at game end. Rewards positioning and timing. Creates natural player interaction through competition for space.

## Uncommon Implementations

These creative approaches to objectives demonstrate alternative design space:

### Hidden Objectives
**Example: Ticket to Ride**
Secret goals only revealed at end. Creates uncertainty about opponents' strategies. Enables surprise victories and bluffing about intentions.

### Variable Victory Conditions
**Example: Root**
Different players have different victory conditions. Asymmetric objectives create unique strategic challenges. Balancing becomes complex but creates variety.

### Simultaneous Objectives
**Example: Dead of Winter**
Personal and group objectives coexist. Must balance cooperation with self-interest. Creates tension and hidden agendas.

### Contracted Objectives
**Example: Trickerion**
Players declare intentions and score based on fulfillment. Commitment to goals before execution. Rewards planning and follow-through.

### Negative Scoring
**Example: The Mind**
Players try to minimize mistakes rather than maximize points. Creates cooperative tension. Different psychological feel than accumulation.

### Relative Scoring
**Example: Mysterium**
Players score based on ranking rather than absolute points. Creates direct comparison. Reduces runaway winners.

## Dimensional Intersections

Objective & Scoring shapes strategic decision-making across all dimensions:

### × Resource Economy
Victory conditions determine which resources are valuable.
- Points for Resources = Direct conversion strategy
- Points for Efficiency = Optimization challenge

### × Progression & Development
Objectives guide which development paths are valuable.
- Points for Engines = Rewards engine building
- Race Condition = Speed vs. quality tradeoff

### × Player Interaction
Victory structure shapes how players compete or cooperate.
- Shared Objectives = Cooperative gameplay
- Zero-Sum Scoring = Direct competition

### × Action Selection
Actions are valued based on their contribution to objectives.
- Multiple Scoring Paths = Flexible action selection
- Focused Objective = Optimized action sequences

## Design Questions

When working with this dimension, consider:

- **How clear should objectives be?** Do players know exactly what to pursue or discover it through play?
- **One path or many?** Does everyone compete the same way or are there multiple viable strategies?
- **When do players score?** Continuously, periodically, or only at game end?
- **Can players see the score?** Is it hidden, partially visible, or completely public?
- **What's the win margin?** Do you want close finishes or clear victories?

## Patterns & Pitfalls

### Successful Patterns

**Milestone + End Game Scoring**
Score points during play for milestones, plus major scoring at end. Creates tension between present and future. Example: Many Euros combine both.

**Diversification Bonuses**
Reward players for breadth rather than specialization. Encourages trying different strategies. Example: Points for variety in collections.

**Multiple Scoring Opportunities**
Many ways to earn points with different efficiency curves. Creates strategic flexibility. Example: Agricola's varied scoring categories.

**Telegraphed Objectives**
Players can see others' goals but not exact details. Creates informed competition without perfect information. Example: Splendor's noble goals.

**Escalating Objectives**
Later objectives are worth more points. Creates building tension and prevents early leads from being decisive. Example: Power Grid.

### Common Pitfalls

**Point Salad Overload**
Everything scores points, no meaningful strategic choices. Players can't evaluate options. Need focused scoring that creates trade-offs.

**Opaque Scoring**
Players can't evaluate who's winning or strategic value of actions. Frustrating and prevents meaningful competition. Need clearer feedback.

**Dominant Strategy**
One approach consistently outscores all others. Reduces strategic variety. Need balanced scoring that rewards different paths.

**Runaway Leaders**
Early scoring advantages compound. Winners are apparent before game ends. Need catch-up mechanics or end-game swing potential.

**Anti-Climactic Endings**
Winner is obvious long before game ends. Players go through motions. Need hidden information or end-game scoring to maintain suspense.

**Kingmaker Situations**
Losing players determine the winner through their final actions. Breaks competitive integrity. Need mechanisms to prevent or accept this.

## Examples in Practice

**Ticket to Ride** uses secret route objectives combined with public longest route bonus. Hidden goals create mystery about opponent strategies while still having clear focus.

**Agricola** pioneered complex multi-category scoring - many different things score points with diminishing returns. Forces strategic trade-offs and diversification. High replayability.

**Azul** has simple, elegant scoring that creates interesting spatial puzzles. Points come from pattern completion. Clear evaluation but subtle optimization.

**7 Wonders** uses multiple scoring categories revealed at game end. Creates surprise victories where second-place player suddenly wins through military or science bonus.

**Pandemic** uses pure cooperative victory - everyone wins or loses together. No individual scoring. Creates shared tension and team problem-solving.

**Race for the Galaxy** combines engine building with race-to-objective. First to 12 cards triggers end, then highest total points wins. Balances speed with quality.

**Splendor** has dual objectives - race to 15 points while also pursuing specific patron cards. Simple but creates interesting timing decisions about when to pivot.
