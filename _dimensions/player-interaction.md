## What This Dimension Represents

Player Interaction defines how players affect each other during play. It answers: **"How do players' choices impact one another?"**

This dimension shapes the social experience at the table. Direct conflict creates tension and drama. Indirect competition keeps focus on personal optimization. Cooperation builds camaraderie. Negotiation sparks conversation and politics.

The level and type of interaction determines whether your game is a shared puzzle, a ruthless battle, or a social negotiation. This choice dramatically affects who enjoys playing and how the game feels socially.

## Common Mechanics

These are the most frequently used approaches to player interaction:

### Area Control / Area Majority
Players compete for dominance over locations or regions. Rewards positioning and timing. Creates shifting alliances and tactical maneuvering. Direct but non-destructive competition.

### Blocking / Denial
Players take actions or resources that prevent opponents from accessing them. Worker placement is classic example. Creates tension through scarcity and competition for opportunities.

### Trading & Negotiation
Players exchange resources, favors, or agreements. Enables deal-making and politics. Can be structured by rules or completely freeform. Creates social gameplay and table dynamics.

### Take That / Direct Conflict
Players use actions or cards that directly harm opponents. Ranges from mild setbacks to elimination. Creates confrontational atmosphere and revenge dynamics.

### Cooperative Goals
Players work together toward shared objectives. May have individual scoring within cooperation. Builds team dynamics and shared problem-solving.

## Uncommon Implementations

These creative approaches to interaction demonstrate alternative design space:

### Hate Drafting
**Example: 7 Wonders, Sushi Go!**
Denying cards to opponents becomes as important as improving your own position. Indirect attack through selection rather than direct conflict.

### Shared Resources
**Example: Evolution**
Common resource pool that all players draw from. Depleting resources hurts everyone but advantages early takers. Creates tragedy of the commons dynamics.

### Positive Externalities
**Example: Suburbia**
Your actions may benefit opponents. Creates interesting decisions about helping others while helping yourself. Subverts typical conflict.

### Variable Alliances
**Example: Cosmic Encounter, Diplomacy**
Players form temporary partnerships that shift over time. Political maneuvering and betrayal become central. High social negotiation.

### Asymmetric Roles with Dependence
**Example: Sheriff of Nottingham**
Different players have different roles with forced interaction. Creates structured social dynamics and role-play opportunities.

### Reactive Abilities
**Example: Race for the Galaxy (expansions)**
Players gain benefits from opponents' actions. Keeps everyone engaged during all turns. Reduces zero-sum competition.

## Dimensional Intersections

Player Interaction shapes the social and competitive dynamics across dimensions:

### × Action Selection
How actions are chosen determines interaction opportunities.
- Worker Placement = Direct blocking and denial
- Simultaneous Selection = Indirect collision and prediction

### × Spatial & Positional
Location creates natural interaction points and territories.
- Area Control = Competing for space
- Network Building = Competing for connections

### × Resource Economy
Resource competition creates interaction through scarcity.
- Shared Market = Competition for purchases
- Trade Economy = Negotiation and exchange

### × Conflict Resolution
How conflicts are resolved shapes interaction feel.
- Dice Combat = Dramatic confrontations
- Deterministic Resolution = Calculated attacks

## Design Questions

When working with this dimension, consider:

- **How direct should interaction be?** Do players attack each other or compete indirectly?
- **Can players be eliminated?** Does losing mean watching others play, or staying relevant?
- **Is interaction symmetrical?** Can all players interact equally, or are some roles more aggressive?
- **How does interaction scale with player count?** Does more interaction become chaotic or engaging?
- **What's the social contract?** Does your game encourage friendly competition or cutthroat play?

## Patterns & Pitfalls

### Successful Patterns

**Graduated Interaction**
Start with low interaction, increase as game progresses. Lets players build positions before conflict. Example: Terra Mystica's late-game competition.

**Multiple Interaction Channels**
Offer different ways to interact - some direct, some indirect. Lets players choose interaction comfort level. Example: Games with both trading and blocking.

**Anti-Runaway Interaction**
Leading players become bigger targets. Balances game through player dynamics. Example: Attack the leader becomes natural strategy.

**Forced Interaction Points**
Require interaction at specific moments rather than constant conflict. Creates drama without constant attacks. Example: Periodic voting or auctions.

### Common Pitfalls

**Kingmaking**
Eliminated or losing players can determine the winner through spite or favoritism. Breaks competitive integrity. Need mechanisms to prevent this.

**Bash the Leader Degeneracy**
Optimal play is always attacking whoever's ahead. Penalizes success and creates stalemate. Need subtler balancing.

**Analysis Paralysis Through Interaction**
Too much interaction makes calculating outcomes impossible. Players can't decide because too many unknowns. Need bounded interaction.

**Negative Player Experience**
Direct conflict feels mean-spirited or personal. Creates bad feelings at table. Need appropriate tone-setting and recovery mechanisms.

**Multiplayer Imbalance**
Game plays very differently at different player counts. Three players may feel completely different from five. Need scaling mechanisms or accept player count limits.

**Solitaire Multiplayer Disappointment**
Players expected social gameplay but everyone just optimizes their own board. False advertising - be clear about interaction level.

## Examples in Practice

**Catan** uses trading as primary interaction - negotiation creates social gameplay without direct attacks. Robber provides mild conflict without being mean. Balanced interaction for casual players.

**Root** has completely asymmetric interaction - each faction interacts differently with others. Creates unique dynamics and relationships. Every player has different enemies and allies.

**Pandemic** is purely cooperative with no hidden agendas. Players solve shared puzzle together. Can suffer from quarterbacking where one player dominates decisions.

**Kemet** embraces direct conflict as core mechanic. Combat is frequent, rewarded, and non-destructive (troops return). Makes fighting feel good rather than punishing.

**Wingspan** is largely solitaire multiplayer with minimal interaction through bird food competition. Appeals to players who want low-conflict optimization. Clear expectations set.

**Sidereal Confluence** is all about negotiation and trading - every turn is simultaneous dealmaking. Trading is literally the game. For social, extroverted players.
