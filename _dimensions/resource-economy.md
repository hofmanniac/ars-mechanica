## What This Dimension Represents

Resource Economy defines the **currency of gameplay** - the things players accumulate, convert, and consume to achieve their goals. It answers: **"What do players acquire and spend?"**

Resources create trade-offs, strategic planning, and tension through scarcity. They're the fuel that powers actions, the constraints that force decisions, and the objectives that drive competition.

Nearly every game has some form of resource economy, whether it's explicit (tokens, coins, cards) or abstract (actions, time, position). The resource systems you design fundamentally shape player strategies and the game's strategic landscape.

## Common Mechanics

These are the most frequently used mechanics for implementing resource economies:

### Resource Tokens
Physical tokens representing commodities (wood, stone, gold). Simple, tangible, and immediately understandable. Enables trading and conversion systems.

### Income Systems
Regular resource generation each turn or phase. Creates predictable growth and long-term planning opportunities. Can scale with development or territory.

### Resource Conversion
Transform one resource type into another at specified rates. Creates optimization puzzles and strategic paths. Enables specialization and trading.

### Resource Management
Limited storage, decay, or consumption requirements. Prevents infinite hoarding and creates pressure to act. Adds urgency to decision-making.

### Multi-Currency Systems
Multiple independent resource types with different uses. Creates complexity and strategic depth through conversion trade-offs and specialization.

## Uncommon Implementations

These creative approaches to resource economy demonstrate alternative design space:

### Time as Resource
**Example: Space Alert, Escape: The Curse of the Temple**
Real-time gameplay where time itself is the limiting resource. Players must optimize actions per second rather than actions per turn.

### Negative Resources
**Example: Mage Knight (wounds), Dominion (curses)**
Resources that hurt you but may be unavoidable. Creates tension between efficiency and contamination. Adds risk/reward calculus to every decision.

### Spatial Resources
**Example: Dominant Species, Scythe**
Territory control generates income or resources. Geography becomes an economic engine. Links spatial strategy to economic power.

### Meta-Resources (Actions as Currency)
**Example: Puerto Rico roles**
Actions or abilities themselves are the scarce resource. No traditional "money" - decisions are the economy.

### Shared Resources
**Example: Flash Point: Fire Rescue**
Cooperative resource pools where players must coordinate spending. Creates consensus-building and group strategy challenges.

### Degrading Resources
**Example: Dead of Winter (morale)**
Resources that decrease over time unless actively maintained. Creates ongoing pressure and maintenance costs.

## Dimensional Intersections

Resource Economy interacts powerfully with several other dimensions:

### × Action Selection
Resources fuel actions or are generated by them. Economy and action systems are tightly coupled.
- Action Points ARE Resources = Strategic budgeting
- Actions Cost Resources = Trade-off decisions

### × Progression & Development
Resources enable upgrades, purchases, and permanent improvements. Economy drives growth curves.
- Resource Income for Upgrades = Snowball effects
- Resource Conversion Efficiency = Engine building

### × Spatial
Territory or position generates resources. Geography becomes economically valuable.
- Area Control Generates Income = Imperial incentive
- Trade Routes = Network economy

### × Player Interaction
Resource scarcity creates competition. Trading enables cooperation or negotiation.
- Competitive Resource Acquisition = Direct conflict
- Trading Mechanics = Negotiation leverage

### × Objective & Scoring
Resources often convert directly to victory points or enable scoring opportunities.
- Resources ARE Victory Points = Direct optimization
- Resources Buy Scoring Cards = Indirect paths

## Design Questions

When designing resource economies, consider:

**Scarcity vs. Abundance**
- Are resources tight and precious, or flowing and plentiful?
- Does scarcity create interesting decisions or frustrating stalls?
- How quickly can players recover from resource deficits?

**Convertibility**
- Can resources be exchanged for each other?
- At what rates? Fixed or market-driven?
- Does conversion create interesting optimization puzzles?

**Sources and Sinks**
- Where do resources come from? (income, actions, territory)
- Where do they go? (actions, purchases, decay)
- Is the economy inflationary, deflationary, or balanced?

**Visibility**
- Are resources public or hidden?
- Can players count opponents' wealth?
- Does information asymmetry add strategy or frustration?

**Storage Limits**
- Can players hoard unlimited resources?
- Do caps create pressure to spend?
- How do limits affect strategic planning?

**Specialization vs. Diversification**
- Should players focus on one resource type or balance many?
- Are there advantages to specialization?
- Do trade-offs favor generalists or specialists?

## Why This Dimension Matters

Resource Economy determines:
- **Strategic Depth**: Complex economies create optimization challenges
- **Pacing**: Resource flow rates control game speed
- **Player Interaction**: Scarcity creates competition; trading creates cooperation
- **Comeback Potential**: Resource systems can enable or prevent catching up
- **Theme**: Resource types convey setting and flavor

## Design Patterns

**Pattern: Exponential Growth**
Resource generation increases with development. Early investments compound over time. Creates engine-building satisfaction but risks runaway leaders.

**Pattern: Resource Pyramid**
Basic resources convert to advanced resources to final products. Creates a progression system and specialization opportunities.

**Pattern: Resource Pressure**
Decay, costs, or requirements force continuous resource management. Prevents passive accumulation and maintains engagement.

**Pattern: Emergency Conversions**
Expensive conversions available when desperate. Provides flexibility but at efficiency cost. Prevents total dead-ends.

**Pattern: Public Goods**
Some resources benefit all players when invested in. Creates cooperation vs. selfish optimization tension.

## Common Pitfalls

**Runaway Economy**
Rich players get richer while poor players fall further behind. Solution: Diminishing returns, catch-up mechanics, or resource caps.

**Starvation**
Players lack resources to do anything meaningful. Solution: Minimum income, multiple resource paths, or reduced costs.

**Optimal Path**
One resource strategy dominates all others. Solution: Balance conversion rates, create situational advantages, or add scarcity.

**Conversion Complexity**
Too many resource types or conversion rules overwhelm players. Solution: Limit types, use intuitive ratios, provide reference cards.

**Economic Stagnation**
Late game becomes repetitive resource grinding. Solution: Exponential growth, variable costs, or alternative victory paths.

**Hoarding**
Optimal play is saving resources indefinitely. Solution: Storage limits, decay, or turn limits that force spending.

## Resource Economy Archetypes

**Tight Economy**
Resources are scarce, every point matters, efficiency is critical. High tension, strategic depth. Risk: frustration if too tight.

**Engine Economy**
Early investments compound into exponential growth. Satisfying progression. Risk: runaway leaders.

**Trading Economy**
Resources gain value through exchange with others. High interaction, negotiation. Risk: kingmaking.

**Conversion Economy**
Multiple paths to transform basic resources into victory. Optimization puzzles. Risk: optimal path dominance.

**Territory Economy**
Control of space generates resources. Links spatial and economic strategy. Risk: snowball from early leads.
